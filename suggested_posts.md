**7. Let us stop treating TVET as a consolation prize**

Every year, after the release of KCSE examination results, thousands of young people and their parents ask one familiar question: did I qualify for university? It is an important question, but perhaps a more useful one would be: what am I good at, and where can my skills take me? For far too long, Kenya's education system and society at large have treated university as the ultimate destination, reducing Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) to a fallback option for those who did not make the grade. That thinking is not only outdated, it is also dangerous, and it overlooks a simple truth: Kenya does not need graduates with certificates alone; it needs people with the competence to solve problems. A country cannot run without skills. Take away plumbers, and water systems will eventually fail. Without electricians, homes, schools, hospitals and industries would be plunged into darkness. Without mechanics, vehicles would remain grounded, and without skilled chefs and hospitality professionals, hotels would struggle to offer the quality of service visitors expect. Without welders, masons, carpenters and construction technicians, architectural drawings would remain nothing more than drawings on paper. Hospitals illustrate the point clearly. Doctors and nurses are indispensable, but hospitals also depend on functioning machines, reliable electrical systems, water supply, refrigeration, oxygen systems, ICT infrastructure and specialised equipment. When machines break down, someone must have the technical knowledge to diagnose the problem, maintain the equipment and restore services. The economy, in other words, runs on both knowledge and skill. This is why the Competency-Based Curriculum, which encourages learners to think about career pathways earlier, should not be dismissed. Every learner is different. Some excel in theoretical and academic work, while others demonstrate exceptional ability in practical problem-solving, technology, engineering, design, agriculture, hospitality, sports or the creative industries. The mistake made for generations has been measuring every child's potential using the same academic ruler, when a learner who may not obtain the grades required for a particular university programme may possess remarkable mechanical ability, an entrepreneurial mind, artistic talent or exceptional technical skills.
