**2. CS Ogamba: New Tertiary Funding Model to focus on students’ careers over parents’ wealth**

Education Cabinet Secretary Julius Migos Ogamba has announced that the government is developing a new tertiary education funding framework that will prioritise students' career aspirations and programme costs rather than the financial status of their parents. Speaking during the University of Embu's 11th graduation ceremony, Ogamba said the proposed Universal Student Funding Model is intended to replace the Student‑Centred Funding Model (SCFM), which was introduced in 2023 but has faced challenges. Under the current system, students are assessed through a Means Testing Instrument that considers the socio‑economic circumstances of parents or guardians before placing them in funding bands. Scholarships and loans are then allocated depending on the band and programme cost, with families expected to meet part of the expenses. Ogamba noted that this approach has disadvantaged students pursuing costly courses such as medicine and engineering, forcing some to switch to cheaper programmes or abandon their studies altogether. Financing will be offered through a mix of scholarships and loans, with repayment expected after graduates secure employment. The proposed Tertiary Education Placement and Funding Bill, 2026, also aims to establish the Tertiary Education Funding Authority (TEFA), which will consolidate funding functions currently handled by different agencies. The framework is expected to mobilise additional resources from government and partners to expand access to tertiary education. Ogamba said the new approach will ensure that students' academic ability and career choices are not limited by their parents' financial circumstances. He added that aligning funding with programme costs will also help address financial challenges facing universities and technical and vocational institutions. The proposal, however, remains subject to parliamentary debate, with questions raised about its sustainability and the extent of government support.

**3. Nairobi introduces new noise permit charges**

The new charges apply to several activities, including weddings, roadshows, construction work, religious activities, clubs and short-term night events. Short-term night events will attract a fee of Ksh100,000. Clubs will also be required to pay annual fees based on their capacity, with charges ranging from Ksh20,000 to Ksh100,000. The cost of a noise permit for weddings will depend on the venue. Weddings held in residential areas will attract a Ksh20,000 fee, while those held in gardens will cost Ksh10,000 per event. Roadshows will be charged depending on the vehicle being used. A saloon car will attract Ksh10,000, a minivan Ksh30,000, while a 24-wheel truck or canter will attract Ksh50,000. The county has also introduced a Ksh20,000 permit for construction activities involving excavation, demolition, drilling and other specified noisy works. Churches and mosques will be required to obtain an annual Noise Emission Control Permit at a cost of Ksh5,000. The new fees come as noise pollution continues to attract attention in Nairobi. The issue has also been taken to court, with Advocate Samwel Barongo Nyamari filing a petition seeking regulation of loud music in public service vehicles. The case names the Matatu Owners Association, NTSA, NEMA and the Attorney General as respondents. The High Court has directed that the petition will be heard next year. The case will seek to address whether matatu operators can play amplified music without the consent of passengers. Under the new rules, failure to comply with some noise permit requirements can result in penalties of up to three times the applicable licence fee. The different charges mean event organisers, religious institutions, businesses and roadshow operators will have to consider noise permit costs when planning their activities. Questions may also arise where an activity falls under more than one category, such as a wedding involving amplified music held at night in a residential area.

**5. Plan to shrink school boards from 17 to nine must not be mistaken for better governance**

Kenya's proposal to reduce school Boards of Management from 17 members to nine has opened a debate that goes far beyond the number of people sitting around a table. It touches on the cost of governance, accountability, teacher representation, learner participation, parental voice, and the role of alumni and alumnae, and ultimately raises the question of whether Kenya is building a more efficient education system or simply rearranging its administrative furniture. The Basic Education (Amendment) Bill, 2025, sponsored by Mathare MP Hon. Anthony Oluoch, has put the proposed restructuring into the parliamentary conversation. The Bill should not be mistaken for an already operational directive requiring schools to immediately replace their existing boards; before any change becomes reality, Parliament must complete its legislative process, and the government must provide clear implementation guidelines. The argument for reform is understandable. The argument for rushed reform is not. The more useful question is not whether nine is smaller than 17, but whether nine people will actually govern a school better than 17, or whether Kenya will simply end up with a smaller board without fixing the problems that affect school governance today. There is nothing inherently wrong with a 17-member board; its size can provide broad representation. But representation can also become unwieldy. Seventeen people may produce longer deliberations, overlapping responsibilities, complicated coordination and higher meeting-related costs, and a smaller board could plausibly make decisions faster with clearer individual accountability. That argument deserves consideration, but it would be intellectually lazy to conclude that nine is automatically better simply because nine is fewer than 17. Nine people can still misuse resources, still exclude the classroom voice, and still fail learners, just as 17 can. The issue, therefore, is not simply size, but composition, competence, accountability and representation. That argument becomes more persuasive when set against how professionally governed organisations operate. A 2025 corporate-governance survey found an average board size of about 11 directors, with nine-member boards among the common configurations, and Safaricom, for instance, has an 11-director board.

**6. Nicotine flavour ban could drive smokers back to cigarettes, survey warns**

NAIROBI, Monday, Aug 24 — Half of Kenyan consumers who use nicotine pouches and vapes say the products help them quit smoking, while nearly one in three say they would return to cigarettes if flavoured products were banned, according to a new survey. The findings come as Parliament considers the Tobacco Control (Amendment) Bill, which proposes new restrictions on smoke-free nicotine products, including a ban on most flavours. The survey, conducted by the Campaign for Safer Alternatives (CASA), found that 50 per cent of respondents use nicotine pouches or vapes to help them quit smoking, while 47 per cent said they use the products because they consider them a less harmful alternative to cigarettes. CASA Chairman Joseph Magero said the findings should prompt lawmakers to reconsider measures that could make it harder for smokers to move away from combustible cigarettes. “Our findings show that the proposed legislation could make it harder for smokers to quit combustible cigarettes. Lawmakers must listen to the voices of smokers who are trying to leave cigarettes behind,” Magero said. The survey also highlighted the role of flavours in helping some users move away from cigarettes. Half of respondents said flavours help them stay off cigarettes entirely, while 43 per cent said flavours help them reduce the number of cigarettes they smoke. Only 14 per cent identified tobacco flavour as their preferred option, with most respondents opting for menthol, fruit or other non-tobacco flavours. Asked how they would respond if flavoured nicotine products were banned, 32 per cent said they would return to smoking, while 18 per cent said they would smoke more. A further 33 per cent said they would seek flavoured products through alternative sources. The survey also found strong support for greater public participation before the proposed legislation is finalised. About 88 per cent of respondents said the Government should hold consultations on the Tobacco Control Bill that are open to the public, while a similar proportion said the concerns of smokers and nicotine-product users were being ignored.
