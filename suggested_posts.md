**1. Water Authority moves to prosecute illegal gold miners poisoning River Yala**

The Water Resources Authority (WRA) has launched a crackdown against illegal gold mining operators contaminating the River Yala basin, warning that offenders face immediate prosecution for ignoring environmental protection orders and deploying deadly toxic chemicals near local water sources. ​Speaking during an enforcement operation in Chisango Village, Kakamega Region, James Nyang'eso, WRA Deputy Director of Regional Coordination, confirmed that unapproved mining activities are severely degrading the river's riparian reserves. ​”Most of these mining activities are being done without any approval from regulatory authorities, including WRA, NEMA, the county government, or the Mining Department,” Nyang'eso said. “Most of these companies are not in our database, making their operations illegal.” ​Nyang'eso stated that WRA previously issued stop-work and land-reclamation orders to operators in the area. However, recent surveillance revealed that miners blatantly defied the directive, continuing intensive operations with heavy machinery, turning land upside down, and stripping native vegetation. ​As a result of the breach, WRA is pursuing charges under Section 93(3) of the Water Resources Regulations. Enforcement will continue until operators fully rehabilitate the degraded land. ​”We are not against the exploitation of mineral resources in this area,” Nyang'eso clarified. “What we oppose is mining without mitigation measures to safeguard our water resources.” ​Water testing across gold-processing sites in the region throughout the week revealed alarming concentrations of toxic chemicals in runoff water. ​Mutahi Jahara, WRA Assistant Director in charge of Water Quality and Pollution Control, highlighted that processing methods near the river utilize mercury and sodium cyanide among the most lethal substances known. ​”We have been testing the runoff from gold leaching plants and found very high concentrations of cyanide,” Jahara said. “When it rains, these toxic chemicals wash straight into our river system.” ​Under Regulation 93(7) of the Water Resources Regulations, commercial gold extraction along protected riparian corridors constitutes a proscribed illegal activity without explicit approval. ​Jahara noted that while small doses of runoff might not cause instant human fatalities, higher concentrations pose an immediate lethal threat to livestock, aquatic life, and local communities.

**2. HELB Opens Applications for Ksh500,000 Loans Targeting Civil Servants**

The Higher Education Loans Board (HELB) has opened applications for education loans of up to Ksh500,000 for civil servants seeking to further their studies and advance their professional qualifications. The financing is being offered through the Civil Servants Training Revolving Fund, which targets employees in the national and county governments, the National Police Service and the Teachers Service Commission. Eligible civil servants can use the loan to pursue PhD, master's, postgraduate diploma and diploma programmes, as well as approved professional courses offered by recognised universities, colleges and other institutions approved by the Ministry of Education. The facility is designed to allow public servants to finance further training while continuing with their employment, with repayment spread over a period of up to six years. "Advance your career with HELB's Civil Servants Training Revolving Fund! Take the next step towards professional growth with quick loan processing, flexible repayment period of 12–72 months, competitive interest rates, and financing of up to KSh 500,000," the board said in a statement. Borrowers will pay an annual interest rate of four per cent, with repayment periods ranging from 12 to 72 months, depending on the amount borrowed and the agreed repayment plan. Repayment will be made through monthly check-off deductions, allowing the loan instalments to be deducted directly from borrowers' salaries. The facility provides civil servants with an alternative source of financing at a lower interest rate and longer repayment period compared with some other education financing options available to salaried workers. The latest facility comes after HELB earlier this month opened applications for the 2026/27 Jielimishe Loan , which targets salaried learners pursuing further education while remaining in employment. Through Jielimishe , eligible employed Kenyans can access up to Ksh600,000 to finance university, postgraduate, professional and other approved higher education programmes. The Jielimishe Loan attracts an annual interest rate of 10 per cent, with repayment periods of between 12 and 48 months through monthly payroll deductions. The programme also provides borrowers with a credit life insurance policy, offering additional financial protection during the repayment period.

But that is much lower than the $100bn it was worth in 2022, reflecting weaker sales growth and higher costs. The initial public offering (IPO) is being backed by Wall Street investment giants Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley and JP Morgan. The company will make its highly anticipated debut on the Hong Kong stock exchange after efforts to go public since 2023. Hong Kong has been revived as “one of the largest IPO markets” after attracting more firms from mainland China, said economics associate professor Feng Qu from the Nanyang Technological University. Shein is likely to command a higher valuation in Hong Kong than it would in London, where regulatory scrutiny derailed its plans to sell shares there, Feng said. Chinese companies may also be wary of selling shares in the US as tensions between the world's two largest economies could result in firms being de-listed, he added. Shein's listing will test investor confidence in the fast-fashion industry and its position in an increasingly competitive market.

**6. From Classrooms to Careers: The Big Promise of Kenya’s New Education Pathways**

Kenya's biggest education revolution may not be happening in government boardrooms. It is happening in homes, where parents are increasingly looking beyond grades and university admission and asking a different question: What is my child genuinely good at? As Competency-Based Education and technical training reshape the system, the country is opening up more routes to success—but turning that promise into reality will require much more than a new curriculum. For decades, education in Kenya was largely viewed through a familiar lens: work hard, score highly in national examinations and secure a place at university. But that conversation is beginning to change. Across many homes, parents are paying closer attention to their children's talents, interests and practical abilities. The focus is gradually expanding beyond the question of which school a child will join to include another important consideration—what kind of future that learner is being prepared for. That changing mindset reflects one of the biggest shifts in Kenya's education sector in a generation. For almost 40 years, the 8-4-4 system defined the educational journey of millions of Kenyans. Academic performance and national examinations carried enormous weight, often becoming the main measure of whether a learner had succeeded. While the system produced generations of professionals, it also left limited space for learners whose strongest abilities did not fit neatly into a traditional academic path. The introduction of the Competency-Based Curriculum, and its later transition into the broader Competency-Based Education framework, was meant to challenge that model. CBE places greater emphasis on developing skills and identifying what learners can do, rather than concentrating primarily on what they can memorise. The first pioneer cohort's move into senior school marked another major step in that transition, with learners expected to pursue pathways aligned with areas such as STEM, Social Sciences, and Arts and Sports Science. The idea is simple: young people do not all have the same strengths, so education should not force every learner to chase exactly the same future. That transition is already visible in the continued rollout of senior school assessment and the wider education reforms shaping learners' opportunities.

Independent Electoral and Boundaries Commission (IEBC) Commissioner An Nderitu has revealed that a university degree is not a requirement to run for public office in the 2027 General Election. Speaking on Sunday, August 23, Nderitu explained that the court ruled that Section 22B(1) of the Elections Act is unconstitutional. She noted that the court found that the degree requirement violated Article 27, which guarantees freedom from discrimination, and Article 10, which provides for public participation. “Before the 2022 general election, there were some people who went to court, and they challenged the requirement for a degree for the president, Member of Parliament, and Governor. The determination of those cases was made after the election, and the verdict was that section 22B (1) of the Elections Act is unconstitutional. “They pegged it on two issues; one of the determinations was that it violated Article 27 and Article 10. Article 27 talks about freedom from discrimination,” Nderitu stated. File image of Ann Nderitu. The IEBC Commissioner said that as the country heads to 2027, there is no requirement for any aspirant to have a university degree. Nderitu mentioned that the requirement can be reintroduced if someone successfully challenges the court decision of IEBC begins fresh public participation. “As we stand today, there is no requirement for a degree for anybody; that is the situation we are in. “Unless maybe somebody goes to court or we start the process of public participation. The Constitution expects that there is some qualification,” Nderitu said. This comes days after IEBC published a timetable outlining key dates and deadlines ahead of the 2027 General Election. In a gazette notice dated Thursday, August 20, IEBC said political parties intending to participate in the election must submit the names of their authorized persons and their specimen signatures to the commission by October 15, 2026.
