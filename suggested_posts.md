On controlled plots the agronomy held up. In Makueni, maize under mulching and microdosing returned Ksh7.41 (USD$0.057) for every shilling spent. Modelling by our research partner put potential erosion reduction up to 85 percent. Now, the part a press release would leave out, is that in Tharaka Nithi, the practice farmers were already using outearned every regenerative treatment we tested. In Kitui, every bean treatment returned less than it cost. Soybean in Embu lost money outright. **6. 100 percent transition, but what about mastery? Kenya must stop moving learners faster than they learn**

Kenya has a difficult education question to answer, and it cannot be answered by blaming one person. When a learner reaches a higher grade unable to read with understanding, write coherently or perform basic arithmetic, who is responsible? Is it the learner who has failed to master the basics? Is it the teacher who has failed to close the gap? Is it the parent who has not provided enough support at home? Or is it government policy that has moved the learner forward without providing a sufficiently strong mechanism for learning recovery? The uncomfortable answer is that all four have responsibilities—but they do not carry the same level of responsibility. The learner must make an effort. Parents must support learning. Teachers must teach effectively and intervene when learners struggle. But government carries the greatest systemic responsibility because it creates the policies, finances public education, determines the curriculum framework and establishes the conditions in which everyone else is expected to perform. That is why Kenya now needs to move beyond the familiar blame game and ask a much more useful question: Where is the learning chain breaking, and what realistic policy can repair it? The 100 per cent transition policy was founded on a noble principle: no child should be pushed out of education simply because of performance or difficult circumstances. Every learner deserves an opportunity to continue learning. That principle should be protected. But access and mastery are not the same thing. A child can transition successfully on paper while still struggling academically. The learner moves from one classroom to another, but the learning gap moves with the learner. A child may reach a higher grade unable to read a simple passage with confidence, understand written instructions, write effectively or perform basic arithmetic. The class changes. The learning problem does not. Beyond repetition versus automatic progression This is where Kenya's education debate becomes serious. Should such a learner automatically repeat? Or should the learner transition because the country has committed itself to universal progression? Neither extreme provides a complete answer.

Others have found refuge in private schools, where they have continued teaching, preparing lessons, assessing learners and managing classrooms, often under modest salaries and without the security and career progression associated with public service. These teachers should not be dismissed as graduates who have merely been waiting for jobs. Many have continued to build valuable classroom experience despite the absence of public employment. The tragedy is that time is slowly working against them. A teacher who qualified several years ago but remains unemployed today may eventually be recruited at an age when only a relatively short period of active service remains before retirement. In other words, prolonged unemployment does not simply deny teachers an income; it consumes part of the very working life for which they were trained. This creates a serious workforce-planning question: Why should Kenya invest in training teachers only to leave them outside classrooms for years and eventually employ some when a significant portion of their productive working years has already passed? Teacher education represents a national investment. Training a teacher requires institutions, lecturers, facilities, teaching practice, examinations and public and private resources. When a qualified teacher remains outside the classroom for years, that investment is not being fully utilised. At the same time, Kenya continues to demand better literacy, numeracy, discipline, creativity and competencies from learners. The contradiction is difficult to ignore. On one hand, the country has trained teachers looking for opportunities. On the other, schools continue to grapple with staffing pressures and large classes in some areas. Teacher shortages remain a live concern before Parliament, including questions to the Education Cabinet Secretary over shortages in particular parts of the country.
